# Survey 2 — generalisation, memorisation, priced penalties, and evidentiary standards (2026-09-09)

**Provenance.** The second of three literature surveys commissioned by the
lead on 2026-09-09 after the fourteenth-pass tally closed. Run by a research
subagent with web search and arXiv verification; the brief described our
setting (a 14B LoRA policy trained on 256 mazes and read on 128–256 held-out
ones, a −10 mine price, an SFT navigator that ignores mines by construction,
a claim that any avoidance is a general habit caused by the price alone, and
the observation that RL barely moves the mine rate and the one responsive run
did not replicate) and asked what a reviewer versed in this literature would
say and what would convince them. Reproduced verbatim; the condensed reading
is `experiments/v2/MZ01_maze_world/record/DIAGNOSIS-2026-09-09.md` §2.2.

**Two citation corrections up front.** (1) "Cobbe et al. 2019 Procgen" is two different papers — *Quantifying Generalization in RL* (arXiv 1812.02341, ICML 2019, CoinRun) and *Procgen* (arXiv 1912.01588, ICML 2020). ## 1. Generalisation vs memorisation in RL-trained LLMs

**SFT Memorizes, RL Generalizes: A Comparative Study of Foundation Model Post-training** (Chu, Zhai, Yang, Tong, Xie, Schuurmans, Le, Levine, Ma; 2025; ICML 2025, arXiv 2501.17161) — On GeneralPoints (arithmetic card game) and V-IRL (navigation), outcome-reward RL generalises to unseen rule and visual variants while SFT memorises the training distribution. This is your headline prior and also your obligation: the paper's evidence is *held-out variants that change the rule*, not just held-out instances, so a reviewer will ask whether your 128–256 unseen mazes are a rule-variant test or merely a fresh sample from the same generator — the latter is the weaker of the two designs Chu et al. ran.

**Does Math Reasoning Improve General LLM Capabilities? Understanding Transferability of LLM Reasoning** (Huan, Li, et al.; 2025; arXiv 2507.00432) — Across 20+ reasoning-tuned models, RL-tuned models transfer across domains while SFT-tuned models drift in representation and output space and forget general capability; sampling distribution, credit assignment and the negative gradient are identified as the drivers of transfer. Directly relevant because your starting point is an SFT navigator: the paper predicts your SFT stage has already induced output-distribution drift toward mine-blind shortest paths, which is a mechanistic reason RL "barely moves the mine rate" rather than an indictment of the price.

**RL's Razor: Why Online Reinforcement Learning Forgets Less** (Shenfeld, Pari, Agrawal, et al.; 2025; NeurIPS 2025, arXiv 2509.04259) — On-policy RL is implicitly biased toward the KL-minimal solution among those that solve the task, which is why it forgets less than SFT. This cuts *against* you here: the same bias that preserves prior knowledge means GRPO will prefer the smallest deviation from the navigator that collects the reward, so a barely-moved mine rate is the predicted outcome of a KL-minimal edit, not evidence the price is inert — measure the KL from the SFT policy on held-out mazes and report it alongside the mine rate.

**Reinforcement Learning Finetunes Small Subnetworks in Large Language Models** (Mukherjee, Yuan, Hakkani-Tür, Peng; 2025; NeurIPS 2025, arXiv 2505.11711) — Across 7 RL algorithms and 10 models, RL updates only 5–30% of parameters, and the subnetworks found from different seeds, data, and algorithms overlap far more than chance. Two implications: your LoRA r=16 is not obviously the binding constraint on capacity, and the greater-than-chance seed overlap gives you a *positive* test — if the price installs a habit, the parameter delta or LoRA subspace should be more similar across your price-on seeds than across price-on vs price-off seeds.

**Echo Chamber: RL Post-training Amplifies Behaviors Learned in Pretraining** (Zhao, Meterez, Kakade, Pehlevan, Jelassi, Malach; 2025; arXiv 2504.07912) — Training from scratch on fully open mixtures, RL fine-tuning amplifies behaviours already present in the pretraining mixture rather than creating new ones. Your SFT navigator "ignores mines by construction", so on this account there may be little mine-avoidance behaviour in the rollout distribution for GRPO to amplify — which is a concrete, testable explanation for the flat mine rate that has nothing to do with the price being wrong.

**Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model?** (Yue, Chen, et al.; 2025; NeurIPS 2025, arXiv 2504.13837) — RLVR-trained models beat their base at pass@1 but are *beaten* by the base at large k; coverage analysis shows the abilities are sampled from, and bounded by, the base model. Run pass@k on your SFT navigator with k in the tens: if mine-free solutions already exist in its sample set, your claim becomes "the price sharpens sampling toward an existing behaviour", which is defensible; if they essentially never appear, the price has nothing to select and no amount of tuning will install the habit.

**Spurious Rewards: Rethinking Training Signals in RLVR** (Shao, Li, Xin, Geng, Wang, Oh, Du, Lambert, Min, Krishna, Tsvetkov, Hajishirzi, Koh, Zettlemoyer; 2025; arXiv 2506.10947) — Random rewards and even *incorrect* labels produce 13.8–21.4% MATH-500 gains on Qwen2.5, comparable to the 29.1% from ground truth, while producing nothing on Llama3.1 and OLMo2. This is the single most dangerous paper for your causal claim: it establishes that an RL gain co-occurring with a reward signal is not evidence the *content* of that signal caused it, so your "nothing else in the pipeline knows about mines" argument must be backed by a run with the mine price randomised or sign-flipped, not merely removed.

**Reasoning or Memorization? Unreliable Results of Reinforcement Learning Due to Data Contamination** (Wu, Ye, et al.; 2025; AAAI 2026, arXiv 2507.10532) — Shows the Qwen2.5 random-reward gains above were largely a contamination artifact, and on a clean generated benchmark (RandomCalculation) only accurate reward signals produce steady improvement. This is the good news for your design: a procedurally generated 9x9 maze pool is the maze-world analogue of RandomCalculation, so contamination is one confound you can argue away cheaply — say so explicitly, it is a real strength.

**Reinforcement Learning for Reasoning in LLMs with One Training Example** (Wang, Yang, et al.; 2025; NeurIPS 2025, arXiv 2504.20571) — 1-shot RLVR on a single well-chosen example recovers most of the gain of a 1.2k-example set, with cross-category generalisation and post-saturation improvement. Your 256-maze pool is therefore very unlikely to be too small to install a *general* habit, which removes "not enough training mazes" as an explanation and points the finger back at the reward geometry or the SFT prior.

**LoRA Without Regret** (Schulman and Thinking Machines Lab; 2025; company blog, not arXiv — cite as a blog post) — LoRA matches full fine-tuning in a broad "low-regret regime" and, for RL specifically, matches full fine-tuning *even at rank 1*, on the argument that policy-gradient methods absorb roughly one bit of information per episode. Pre-empts the reviewer objection that r=16 is too small to install a habit, but the same one-bit-per-episode argument tells you your total information budget is roughly (episodes x 1 bit), so report your episode count — a habit installed from a few thousand bits is a strong claim.

## 2. Generalisation in deep RL proper

**Quantifying Generalization in Reinforcement Learning** (Cobbe, Klimov, Hesse, Kim, Schulman; 2019; ICML 2019, arXiv 1812.02341) — Introduces CoinRun and shows agents overfit badly to small level sets, with the recommendation to train on ~500 procedurally generated levels and test on held-out ones because that is near where generalisation begins to take effect. Your 256-maze training pool sits *below* the threshold this paper identifies as the onset of generalisation, which is the most concrete, citable criticism of your setup — a training-pool sweep (64 / 256 / 1024 mazes, same compute) is the natural response and would itself be a publishable curve.

**Leveraging Procedural Generation to Benchmark Reinforcement Learning** (Cobbe, Hesse, Hilton, Schulman; 2020; ICML 2020, arXiv 1912.01588) — The Procgen suite of 16 procedurally generated environments, established as the standard train-on-N-levels / test-on-held-out protocol for measuring RL generalisation. Adopt its reporting convention verbatim — train and test curves on the same axes, generalisation gap as the headline number — since it is what reviewers in this area read fluently.

**A Study on Overfitting in Deep Reinforcement Learning** (Zhang, Vinyals, Munos, Bengio; 2018; arXiv 1804.06893) — In a 2D gridworld where the agent collects positive-reward objects and avoids negative-reward ones, agents can memorise random mazes wholesale, and injected stochasticity (sticky actions, random starts) neither prevents nor reveals the overfitting. This is almost exactly your environment with your reward structure, and its warning is precise: sampling temperature and epsilon-greedy exploration will *not* substitute for a held-out set as evidence against memorisation.

**A Survey of Zero-shot Generalisation in Deep Reinforcement Learning** (Kirk, Zhang, Grefenstette, Rocktäschel; 2023; JAIR 76:201–264, arXiv 2111.09794) — Formalises ZSG, gives the context-MDP framing, and taxonomises benchmarks and methods by what kind of distribution shift they test. Use its vocabulary to state precisely what you are claiming: with a fixed generator, unseen mazes are an *IID-generalisation* claim, which is strictly weaker than the OOD claim ("a general habit") your framing implies — either soften the wording or add a shifted test set (larger grids, higher mine density, mines in configurations absent from training).

**Reinforcement Learning with Augmented Data** (Laskin, Lee, Stooke, Pinto, Abbeel, Srinivas; 2020; NeurIPS 2020, arXiv 2004.14990) **and Automatic Data Augmentation for Generalization in Deep RL** (Raileanu, Goldstein, Yarats, Kostrikov, Fergus; 2020/2021; NeurIPS 2021, arXiv 2006.12862) — Simple augmentations (crop, cutout, translate) and their automatically-selected, regularised variants improve Procgen test performance by ~40% relative to standard RL. For ASCII grids the pixel augmentations do not port, but the symmetry group does: rotations and reflections of a 9x9 maze are free label-preserving augmentation and would let you multiply your 256-maze pool by 8 toward Cobbe's 500-level threshold without generating new mazes.

## 3. Habit vs policy: priced penalties, over-caution, and the completion trade-off

**Constrained Policy Optimization** (Achiam, Held, Tamar, Abbeel; 2017; ICML 2017, arXiv 1705.10528) — The first general-purpose policy search algorithm for constrained RL with near-constraint-satisfaction guarantees each iteration, replacing a hand-tuned penalty with an explicit constraint. The relevant point for you is structural: a fixed price of -10 is the thing CPO exists to *avoid*, because a scalar penalty has no mechanism to hold mine rate at a target while protecting solve rate — which is precisely the failure you observed when the one responsive run collapsed.

**Benchmarking Safe Exploration in Deep Reinforcement Learning** (Ray, Achiam, Amodei; 2019; OpenAI technical report — Safety Gym; no arXiv id, cite as a tech report) — Introduces PPO-Lagrangian and TRPO-Lagrangian, which adapt the penalty coefficient online via a dual variable and outperform CPO on constraint satisfaction in Safety Gym, with the standard reporting being episodic return *and* cost together plus a final cost-rate. The immediate practical upgrade to your pipeline: make the -10 a Lagrange multiplier targeting a mine rate (say 2%), which converts your brittle price-tuning problem into a constrained one and gives you the return-vs-cost frontier that this literature expects to see.

**The general penalty-tuning finding in safe RL** (multiple sources, converging; see the CPO and Safety Gym lines above and 2024–2025 follow-ups such as *Conservative and Adaptive Penalty for Model-Based Safe RL*, arXiv 2112.07701, and *Safe But Not Sorry: Reducing Over-Conservatism in Safety Critics via Uncertainty-Aware Modulation*, 2025, arXiv 2510.18478 — I have not verified the latter's author list, so cite by title and year) — The recurring, near-universal statement is that fixed penalty factors yield either persistent violations (too small) or over-conservative policies with poor task completion (too large), with high sensitivity to the coefficient. Your two observations — "barely moves the mine rate" and "when it moves, solve rate collapses" — are *the textbook signature of this regime*, which means the honest framing is that -10 lands on a knife edge, and a penalty sweep (-2, -5, -10, -20, -50) plotted as mine-rate vs solve-rate is the single most convincing figure you could produce.

**AI Safety Gridworlds** (Leike, Martic, Krakovna, Ortega, Everitt, Lefrancq, Orseau, Legg; 2017; arXiv 1711.09883) — A suite of gridworlds for safe interruptibility, side effects, reward gaming, safe exploration and distributional shift, each with a *hidden* performance function distinct from the observed reward, so that reward-maximising and safe behaviour can be told apart. Adopt this design: your paper needs a hidden metric the policy is not optimising — e.g. detour length, or mine-adjacency rate on mazes where the shortest path is already mine-free — because mine rate alone cannot distinguish a learned avoidance habit from a policy that merely got shorter or more timid.

**Defining and Characterizing Reward Hacking** (Skalse, Howe, Krasheninnikov, Krueger; 2022; NeurIPS 2022 — proceedings title reads "Reward Gaming"; arXiv 2209.13085) — Formalises "unhackable" proxies and proves that over the set of all stochastic policies, two reward functions can be mutually unhackable only if one is constant, so a proxy that never trades off against true performance essentially does not exist. Implies you cannot tune your way to a price that raises mine-avoidance without ever costing solve rate; the paper licenses you to *report* the trade-off as inherent rather than treat the collapsed run as a bug.

**The Effects of Reward Misspecification: Mapping and Mitigating Misaligned Models** (Pan, Bhatia, Steinhardt; 2022; ICLR 2022, arXiv 2201.03544) — Builds four environments with misspecified rewards and finds more capable agents exploit the misspecification more, with *phase transitions* where crossing a capability threshold makes behaviour shift qualitatively and true reward drop sharply. This names your failure mode: the single run where the mine rate moved and the solve rate later collapsed looks like a phase transition, not noise, so plot mine rate and solve rate against training step for that run — a sharp joint transition is a real finding and worth reporting as one.

## 4. Establishing that the *price* caused it: seeds, ablations, statistics

**Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters** (Henderson, Islam, Bachman, Pineau, Precup, Meger; 2018; AAAI 2018, arXiv 1709.06560) — Varying only the random seed produces non-overlapping learning curves, and averaging two disjoint splits of 5 seeds can yield curves that look like different distributions; every deep RL paper they surveyed used 5 seeds or fewer. Your "did not replicate at the same seed" observation is the canonical Henderson result, and it means a single responsive run out of several is, by this paper's standard, zero evidence — it is the finding a reviewer will reach for first.

**How Many Random Seeds? Statistical Power Analysis in Deep Reinforcement Learning Experiments** (Colas, Sigaud, Oudeyer; 2018; arXiv 1806.08295) — Gives explicit power-analysis guidance for the t-test and bootstrap CI test relating seed count to Type-I/Type-II error rates, and to the effect size one wants to detect. This is your answer to "how many seeds is enough": do the calculation from your observed between-seed SD in mine rate and the smallest mine-rate drop you would call a habit — for typical RL variance and a few-percentage-point effect this lands in the 10–20 seeds-per-arm range, not 3.

**Deep Reinforcement Learning at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice** (Agarwal, Schwarzer, Castro, Courville, Bellemare; 2021; NeurIPS 2021 Outstanding Paper, arXiv 2108.13264; library: `rliable`) — Point estimates of aggregate performance hide the uncertainty from few runs; the paper prescribes stratified bootstrap confidence intervals, interquartile mean instead of mean or median, and performance profiles. This is the concrete presentation format for your claim — report IQM of mine rate with stratified bootstrap CIs for price-on vs price-off, and a probability-of-improvement between the two arms, which is exactly the statistic that survives few seeds.

**Empirical Design in Reinforcement Learning** (Patterson, Neumann, White, White; 2024; JMLR 25, arXiv 2304.01315) — A book-length treatment of the statistical assumptions behind RL performance measures, characterising variation and stability, hypothesis testing, multiple-agent comparison, and — critically — handling hyperparameters and experimenter bias. Its hyperparameter section is the one to read before you finalise: if the price-on arm got any tuning the price-off arm did not, the comparison is confounded, and this is the reference a reviewer will cite when they say so.

**A Sober Look at Progress in Language Model Reasoning: Pitfalls and Paths to Reproducibility** (Hochlehnert, Bhatnagar, Udandarao, Albanie, Prabhu, Bethge; 2025; arXiv 2504.07086) — Reasoning benchmarks are highly sensitive to decoding parameters, random seeds, prompt formatting, and even hardware/software configuration; under a standardised evaluation framework, RL gains shrink far below reported claims and show clear overfitting on small benchmarks. Your 128–256-maze evaluation is a small benchmark by exactly this argument — fix and report the decoding temperature, evaluate over multiple sampling seeds per checkpoint, and give the binomial CI on the mine rate, which at n=128 is roughly ±5 percentage points and may already be wider than the effect you are chasing.

**Understanding R1-Zero-Like Training: A Critical Perspective** (Liu, Chen, et al.; 2025; arXiv 2503.20783; introduces Dr. GRPO) — Identifies an optimisation bias in GRPO's normalisation that artificially inflates response length, particularly for incorrect responses, and supplies an unbiased variant. Since you run GRPO-style updates, check whether the standard normalisation is biasing your advantage estimates given your reward scale (-10 mine, -2 step, +20 goal): the group-relative baseline interacts with a large-magnitude rare penalty, and a mine hit's advantage can be swamped or amplified depending on group composition, which is a plausible mechanical reason the price fails to propagate.

**AlphaMaze: Enhancing Large Language Models' Spatial Intelligence via GRPO** (Dao, Vu, et al.; 2025; arXiv 2502.14669) — The closest published analogue to your pipeline: tokenised maze, SFT to predict movement tokens (86.0% on MazeBench), then GRPO on top, with baseline models at 0%. Cite it as the setup precedent and as your novelty boundary — they do the SFT-then-GRPO maze recipe but not the priced-hazard question, so your contribution is the mine price and its transfer, which sharpens what your paper is actually about.

## Five-line summary

A reviewer versed in this literature would say your result currently shows **nothing about mine-avoidance yet**: one responsive run that failed to replicate at the same seed is the textbook Henderson et al. artifact, and at n=128 held-out mazes your binomial CI is likely wider than the effect.

They would add that the flat mine rate is **over-determined by known mechanisms** — RL's Razor predicts a KL-minimal edit to a mine-blind SFT prior, Echo Chamber and Yue et al. predict RL can only amplify what the navigator already samples, and 256 training mazes sits below Cobbe et al.'s ~500-level threshold where generalisation begins.

They would say the collapse in the one responsive run is **not a bug but the signature of a fixed scalar penalty** — the exact failure CPO and PPO-Lagrangian exist to fix, and the phase-transition shape Pan et al. document.

To be convinced of causation they would want: **10–20 seeds per arm** sized by Colas et al.'s power analysis, IQM with stratified bootstrap CIs and probability-of-improvement per rliable, and — because of Spurious Rewards — a *shuffled/sign-flipped* mine price arm, not just a price-off arm.

To be convinced of a **habit** they would want a penalty sweep tracing the mine-rate/solve-rate frontier, an OOD test (larger grids, unseen mine densities) rather than fresh IID samples, a hidden performance metric per AI Safety Gridworlds so avoidance is distinguishable from timidity, and pass@k on the SFT navigator to show the behaviour was there to be selected.
